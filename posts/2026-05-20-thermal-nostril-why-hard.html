Why Nostril Detection on Thermal is Fundamentally Harder than on RGB — and Four Ways Around It

RGB has skin texture; thermal does not. A small dark blob inside a face on thermal looks like every other small dark blob — anywhere on the face, anywhere on the room. This post unpacks why off-the-shelf models fail on thermal nostrils (per the bake-off in part 1) and proposes four research-grade fixes: anatomical priors as a regulariser, paired-RGB label projection, T-FAKE synthetic pretraining, and using the breath-rate signal itself as self-supervision.

The bake-off in part 1 showed that off-the-shelf RGB-trained models fall apart on thermal nostril detection — Sapiens2-0.4b produced ~72 px mean error on thermal vs ~5 px on RGB; even MediaPipe FaceMesh was off by 18 px. This post argues that the failure is not just a matter of “more training data on thermal” — it is partly an information problem in the thermal modality itself, and proposes four concrete routes around it.

This is part 4 of the thermal nostril series (parts 2 and 3 cover few-shot finetuning and hierarchical RGB detection respectively).

The information problem

A nostril keypoint is a specific anatomical point — the lateral edge of the nasal alae. On an RGB face there are multiple co-located cues that pin it down:

  1. Tonal boundary between skin and the opening of the nostril, which is darker because it’s a small cavity.
  2. Specular highlight on the convex side of the alae from any directional light source.
  3. Skin micro-texture (pores, lashes near the nose root) that gives the model a stable local descriptor.
  4. Colour cue — the inside of the nostril is often a different chromaticity (more red) from the surrounding skin.

A pretrained model has implicitly learned to combine these. Remove them all and you are left with thermal — where the signal is temperature, and where the dominant cue for a nostril is:

A small region that is several degrees cooler than the surrounding skin during inhalation, and warmer than the surrounding skin during exhalation.

That description has two problems.

Problem 1 — Thermal nostrils look like every other small cool spot

A nostril on thermal looks like a small low-intensity blob ~5-15 px across. So does:

  • The mouth opening (especially when relaxed-open).
  • The eyebrow shadow line in some subjects.
  • A small bead of sweat that has just evaporated and cooled the skin underneath.
  • The inside of the ear canal.
  • A patch where the subject leant against a cool surface 30 seconds earlier (heat dissipation lag).
  • Any small distractor in the background that happens to be cool — a USB-C port, a coffee mug, a dark interior of a half-open door.

The model has no photometric way to distinguish these cases. Sapiens2 and MediaPipe both demonstrate this exact failure on the bake-off samples — Sapiens2 puts its nostril predictions on the ears (cool because they’re poorly perfused), MediaPipe puts them in the middle of the upper lip area (cool because the philtrum has less subcutaneous fat).

The fundamental ambiguity: in a single thermal frame, “small cool blob within face region” does not uniquely identify nostrils.

Problem 2 — The signal is temporal, not spatial

In a single thermal frame, both nostrils are often almost-invisible (especially mid-breath, when temperature has equalised). The cue that makes nostrils stand out — the periodic warm-cool oscillation as a subject breathes — only exists across time.

This means single-frame thermal models are fundamentally working against the modality. A static thermal frame of a sleeping subject has almost no information about where the nostrils are until you compare it to a frame 2 seconds later.

Problem 3 — Resolution and scale

Most thermographic cameras have native resolutions of 320×240 to 640×480 pixels. At a normal seated distance, a face is ~150×200 px, and a single nostril alae is ~3-5 px wide. That is right at or below the spatial-feature scale that most ViT-style backbones can resolve (ViT patches are usually 14-16 px). Sapiens2 at 0.4B works at a 1024×768 input — but its receptive field for the first attention layer is still 16 px, larger than the entire feature we’re trying to localise.

Why the bake-off looked the way it did

Bringing all this back to the actual experimental results:

  • Sapiens2 (72 px) has the most parameters and the most keypoints, but its pretraining bias is fine-grained skin texture. With that signal absent on thermal, it relies on the next-most-discriminative cue — face outline — and snaps its 308-keypoint template to whichever bright/dark edges the thermal frame happens to expose. On most subjects those edges are at the ears or hairline, hence the systematic ear-direction bias.
  • DWPose (8 px) is closer to a coincidence than competence. Its top-down RTMPose-style design first does a coarse face localisation that does work on thermal (because the face-vs-background distinction is high-contrast on thermal — the body is hot, the background is cool). Once the face crop is right, the nose-tip block is fed a 256×256 patch where the nostrils are the only sub-face-size dark spots, and the model lands on them ~80% of the time.
  • MediaPipe (18 px) is also doing a coarse-then-fine cascade (face detection → mesh fitting). The mesh has 478 vertices, several near the nostrils, but the fitted mesh has no anatomical knowledge of where on the nose tip the alae actually are on a thermal face. It settles ~18-20 px above the alae centre, in the middle of the broader low-temperature nose region.

In short: the only models that worked were the ones that already had a coarse face detector providing geometric prior. That is the key insight, and it points directly at the first fix below.

Four ways forward

Fix 1 — Lean harder on anatomical priors (hierarchical detection)

Treat nostril detection as the last stage of a hierarchical pipeline that has already done all the easy localisations:

  1. Detect the head outline (this works on thermal — body vs background is a strong intensity contrast).
  2. Detect the eye region (the periorbital area is hotter than surrounding skin; the boundary is a strong feature).
  3. Snap a coarse face template that places the nose midway between the eyes, at roughly 1/3 of the face’s vertical extent.
  4. Only inside that 30×30 px region, find the two coolest local minima — those are the nostrils.

This converts an ill-posed dense prediction problem into a well-posed constrained one. DWPose’s accidental win in the bake-off is essentially because it already does steps 1–3 implicitly via its RTMDet face detector.

Part 3 of this series shows the version of this for RGB images (where the same hierarchical principle holds, just with different per-stage signals). The same architecture applies on thermal — the per-stage primitives change, but the principle of “small landmarks only after large landmarks are pinned down” is universal.

Fix 2 — Project labels from paired RGB → thermal

SF-TL54 itself shows you a free supervision signal: every thermal frame has a paired RGB frame, captured by a co-mounted RGB sensor with known extrinsic calibration. The RGB frame is where MediaPipe / Sapiens2 etc. work reliably.

paired_rgb -> MediaPipe FaceMesh -> 478 RGB landmarks
              |
              v
       extrinsic homography (RGB cam -> thermal cam)
              |
              v
              -> 478 *thermal-coordinate* landmarks (free GT)

If the two cameras are aligned to within a few pixels — a calibration that takes 20 minutes with a checkerboard target — you get labelled thermal frames for free, at the rate your RGB camera produces them. You can then bootstrap a thermal-specific model from any RGB-paired sequence — no human labelling, no manual landmark clicks.

This is exactly the approach that made TFD68 (SIGGRAPH Asia 2025) feasible — 28k thermal images annotated by projection from a high-quality RGB landmarker.

Fix 3 — Pretrain on synthetic thermal (T-FAKE)

The T-FAKE dataset generates 100k synthetic thermal faces from a parametric head model, with known nostril positions in the thermal frame. The synthetic-to-real gap on thermal is much smaller than it is on RGB (no chromatic information to mismatch, no specular highlights to model wrong). A model pretrained on T-FAKE and finetuned on ~50 real thermal frames substantially outperforms one trained from scratch on either alone.

This is the most data-efficient route in practical terms — the synthetic data is free, the small real set is what makes the predictions calibrated.

Fix 4 — Use breath-rate as self-supervision

The most interesting route in my view. The whole reason to localise nostrils on thermal is to measure the temperature oscillation — that periodic signal is the entire pipeline output. So:

  1. Run a coarse face detector to localise the head region.
  2. For every pixel inside the face region, compute the temporal FFT magnitude in the 0.1–0.4 Hz band (the breathing-rate band).
  3. The pixels with the strongest periodicity in that band are the nostrils, by definition.

This is “annotate the data the way you would have measured it anyway”. No GT clicks, no synthetic data, no paired RGB needed — just a 10-second clip of the subject breathing. Add this as a self-supervised loss on top of whatever spatial model you train, and you get a model that is biased toward landmarks that are physically meaningful for the downstream task rather than landmarks that are visually salient on a static frame.

The downside: requires video, not single frames. The upside: video is what you’d be processing anyway for breath-rate monitoring, so the cost is zero relative to the deployment scenario.

What I’d reach for first in practice

If I had to ship a thermal-nostril detector tomorrow:

Constraint Pick Why
No labels, no time, single thermal frames DWPose Works zero-shot well enough. Median ~8 px.
30 labelled frames available, want best single-frame model Finetune a tiny head on Sapiens2 backbone (part 2 of this series) Backbone features transfer; only the head needs to be re-aimed.
Paired thermal + RGB streams available Project from RGB MediaPipe + use thermal-only model for failure cases Free labels, lowest engineering cost.
Need true breath-rate signal Temporal FFT band-pass on every face pixel Avoids the localisation problem entirely.

The four fixes are not mutually exclusive. The strongest single-frame pipeline almost certainly combines (1) anatomical-prior hierarchical detection with (3) T-FAKE synthetic pretraining + a small fraction of (2) paired-RGB labels for real-domain calibration. Add (4) on top during deployment when you have video.

What this post is not doing

I haven’t measured any of the four fixes here. This post is a framework for thinking about the problem informed by the experimental finding in part 1 that off-the-shelf models fail. Parts 2 and 3 show working implementations of fixes 1 and parts of 3. Fix 4 (breath-rate self-supervision) is the most novel of the four and I don’t have a working implementation yet — let me know if you want to collaborate on it.
